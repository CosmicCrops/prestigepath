Three Phases. One Goal: Your Independence.

The three phases follow your benefits — not a sales funnel. As your work grows, your benefits move through stages Social Security built for exactly this journey: protection first, adjustment second, independence last. These phases are also how Prestige Path gets paid — we only reach our milestones when you reach yours, so our incentives point the same direction as yours. Phase 1 — Protected: Your Trial Work Period

If you receive SSDI, your first work phase is fully protected. During your nine Trial Work months — in 2026, any month you earn over $1,210 counts as one — you keep your full check no matter how much you earn. This is the test-drive phase, and it’s where our front-door help lives: we build your plan, send you real job leads with direct links, point you to job fairs, and connect you with WorkSource and career centers. But hear this plainly: that help only gets you started. Nobody lands a job by waiting — the applying, showing up, and following through is yours, in every phase.You’re in Phase 1 if: you still have Trial Work months left — or you haven’t started working yet.

2

Phase 2 — Adjusting: Your Extended Period of Eligibility

Once your Trial Work months are used, you enter the 36-month Extended Period of Eligibility — and now your earnings steer the check. Months at or below SGA ($1,690 a month in 2026) are paid; months above are paused, never terminated outright, and your first above-SGA month comes with a three-month grace period. This is the phase where self-motivation pays off most: steady work moves you forward, and if earnings dip, your benefits restart without a new application. We check in as your hours and pay change, so you always know which side of the line you’re on.You’re in Phase 2 if: your Trial Work months are used up and you’re working, with your check adjusting month to month.

3

Phase 3 — Independent: Benefits Behind You, Net Beneath You

Phase 3 is the goal: your earnings have replaced your benefits. And it is not a cliff edge. If your disability stops you from working at that level within five years, Expedited Reinstatement can restart your benefits without a brand-new application — with up to six months of provisional payments while Social Security decides. Your Medicare runs at least 93 months past your Trial Work Period. We stay in touch through this phase too, because independence you can keep beats independence that collapses.You’re in Phase 3 if: your benefits ended because of your earnings — and you’re building a work life that lasts.

On SSI? Your payment adjusts gradually from your first paycheck instead of moving through Trial Work and EPE stages — the SSI section of the FAQ below breaks down your math.

SSDI — Your Check and Your Job

The Trial Work Period is SSDI’s built-in test drive. You get nine Trial Work months within a rolling 60-month window, and during those months you keep your full SSDI check no matter how much you earn — as long as you report your work and still have your disabling condition. In 2026, a month counts as a Trial Work month if your gross earnings top $1,210 (or you work more than 80 hours in self-employment). The months do not have to be consecutive.

After your Trial Work Period, the key number is Substantial Gainful Activity — SGA. In 2026, SGA is $1,690 a month in gross earnings for non-blind beneficiaries, and $2,830 if you’re blind (figures from the SSA’s 2026 Red Book; they adjust most years). During your 36-month Extended Period of Eligibility, you receive your check for months you earn at or below SGA, and it’s paused for months above SGA — it is not an all-or-nothing cliff.

The Extended Period of Eligibility (EPE) is the 36-month safety window that starts right after your Trial Work Period. For months your earnings are at or below SGA, your SSDI check is paid; for months above SGA, it’s suspended — not terminated. The first month Social Security finds you worked above SGA, you still get paid for that month plus the next two — that’s the grace period. And if your earnings drop back below SGA during the EPE, your benefits restart without a new application.

Two safety nets have you. First, during your Extended Period of Eligibility, benefits restart for any month earnings fall to SGA or below — no new application. Second, Expedited Reinstatement: if your benefits ended because of work and, within five years, your disability stops you from working at that level, you can ask Social Security to restart your benefits without a brand-new application — and you can receive up to six months of provisional payments while they decide. Trying to work is not a one-way door.

Yes — always, and promptly. Report when you start or stop a job, your gross earnings, and any changes in hours or pay, and keep your pay stubs. Timely reporting is the single best protection against overpayments, because Social Security adjusts your benefits based on what you report. You can report through your my Social Security account, by phone, or at your local office — and as your Employment Network, we’ll help you build the habit and keep the paperwork straight.

Don’t ignore the notice — but don’t panic, either. An overpayment letter explains what Social Security believes happened, and you have options: you can ask for a full explanation, appeal if the amount or the decision is wrong, or request a waiver if the overpayment wasn’t your fault and repaying it would cause hardship. Free help exists to understand the letter before you respond — a WIPA counselor through the Ticket to Work Help Line (1-866-968-7842), or us. Deadlines matter, so open the envelope and get help early.

SSI — Your Payment and Your Job

On SSI, working is math that favors you. Social Security first ignores the first $20 of income and the first $65 you earn each month, then counts only half of what’s left against your payment. A real example with 2026 figures: earn $400 in a month and only $157.50 counts — so a maximum SSI payment of $994 becomes $836.50, and your total income is $1,236.50 instead of $994. There is no earnings cliff in SSI: every paycheck leaves you with more total money than not working.

Usually, yes. Under rule 1619(b), you can keep Medicaid even if your earnings grow high enough to bring your SSI cash payment to $0 — as long as you still have your disabling condition, you need Medicaid in order to work, and your earnings stay below your state’s 1619(b) threshold (thresholds vary by state and are updated yearly). For many people, keeping Medicaid removes the single biggest risk of going back to work.

Yes — the Student Earned Income Exclusion. If you’re under 22 and regularly attending school, Social Security excludes your earnings from the SSI calculation up to $2,410 a month, with a yearly cap of $9,730 in 2026. That means many working students keep their full SSI payment while they earn — one of the strongest work incentives in either program.

A Plan to Achieve Self-Support (PASS) lets you set aside income or resources for a specific work goal — training, equipment, a vehicle, tools, even starting a business — without that money counting against your SSI eligibility. The plan must be written and approved by Social Security, and the goal has to lead toward supporting yourself through work. It’s underused and powerful, and it’s one of the first things we can explore together.

Health Coverage and Medical Reviews

Not for a long time. If you’re on SSDI, your premium-free Medicare Part A continues for at least 93 months — seven years and nine months — after your Trial Work Period ends, even if your cash benefits stop because of earnings, as long as your disabling condition continues. Part B and Part D can continue too if you stay enrolled and pay those premiums. And after the 93 months, a buy-in option exists. Your health coverage is built to outlast your first years back at work.

Here’s a protection most people never hear about: while your Ticket is assigned and you’re actively making progress toward your employment goal, Social Security will not schedule a medical Continuing Disability Review (CDR). Choosing to try work through Ticket to Work pauses the medical-review risk that stops so many people from trying at all.

Impairment-Related Work Expenses are things you pay for out of pocket, because of your disability, that you need in order to work — examples include specialized transportation, attendant care, assistive technology, and certain medical costs. Social Security can deduct IRWE from your countable earnings, which can keep an SSDI check going during your eligibility period or increase your SSI payment. Keep your receipts: documentation is everything, and it’s one of the first things we look at in a BWIA session.
